# Temporal PLR DML for Time Series

## Introduction

Chapters 1–4 developed DML for cross-sectional data where observations are independent. However, many economic questions involve **time series data** where observations exhibit temporal dependence. This chapter develops the companion repo's current time-series implementation: scalar partially linear DML with lagged treatment controls, temporal cross-fitting, and HAC inference.

<ResultBox>

The class used in this chapter, `TemporalPLRDML`, is a _scalar_ partially linear estimator: it estimates one treatment effect after controlling for current covariates and lagged treatments, and does not estimate period-specific $\theta_t$ effects. Recursive dynamic g-estimation with period-specific blips is implemented separately as `DynamicGEstimationDML` in the next chapter.

</ResultBox>

<ResultBox>

**Chapter objectives:**

- Understand why standard cross-fitting fails with time series data.
- Implement time series cross-validation with purging and blocking.
- Derive HAC (Newey–West) standard errors for autocorrelation-robust inference.
- Apply TemporalPLRDML to estimate scalar temporal PLR effects.
- Use simulations as implementation checks, not proof of universal validity.

</ResultBox>

### The time series challenge

Consider estimating the effect of a policy intervention $T_t$ on an outcome $Y_t$:

$$
Y_t = \tau \cdot T_t + g(X_t, T_{t-1}, \ldots, T_{t-L}) + \varepsilon_t
$$

Two features of time series matter for this example.

1. **Temporal ordering.** A shuffled fold can train the nuisance models on observations from after the date being scored. Forward folds use only information available by that date.
2. **Uncertainty in the treatment effect.** The PLR influence score multiplies the residual treatment by the residual outcome error and scales by the treatment residual's second moment. Serial dependence in those scores can change the standard error.

<InsightBox>

Examine the out-of-fold influence scores in time order. The outcome error can be persistent even when the treatment innovation makes the score uncorrelated across dates. The simulations below show how this distinction affects interval coverage.

</InsightBox>

## Time series cross-validation

### The data leakage problem

Standard K-fold cross-validation randomly assigns observations to folds. With time series data, this means the model predicting $Y_{100}$ might train on $\{Y_{99}, Y_{101}, Y_{102}\}$ — data that would be unavailable in a real forecasting scenario.

<Theorem kind="example" n="5.1" name="Data Leakage Illustration" id="ex-data-leakage">

Consider a 10-observation time series with 2-fold CV.

**Random splitting** (wrong for time series):

```
Fold 1: Train on {t=2,3,5,7,9}, Test on {t=1,4,6,8,10}
Fold 2: Train on {t=1,4,6,8,10}, Test on {t=2,3,5,7,9}
```

When testing $t=4$, the model trains on $t=5,7,9$ — **future data**.

**Temporal splitting** (correct):

```
Fold 1: Train on {t=1,2,3,4,5}, Test on {t=6,7,8,9,10}
```

Now testing never uses future training data.

</Theorem>

### Time series cross-validation strategies

<Theorem kind="remark" name="Companion v1.1.0">

As of companion release 1.1.0, the temporal-validation infrastructure in this chapter — cross-validation splitters, HAC/Newey–West inference, and stationarity diagnostics — is consumed from the `temporalcv` library rather than maintained inside the companion. Each migration was gated on golden snapshots of the estimators' numerical output; the one deliberate behavioral change (the forward-only purged walk replacing a bidirectional purged K-fold) is a leakage *correction*, documented where it appears below.

</Theorem>

Three strategies are available (implemented upstream in `temporalcv`; the companion's estimators select among them via `cv_strategy`):

<Theorem kind="definition" n="5.1" name="Expanding Window CV" id="def-expanding-cv">

For $K$ folds with gap $g$ and test size $m$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Fold } k: \quad &\text{Train} = \{1, \ldots, n_k\} \\
                      &\text{Test} = \{n_k + g + 1, \ldots, n_k + g + m\}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $n_k = n_{\min} + k \cdot \Delta$ grows with each fold. The training window **expands** over time, using all available historical data.

</Theorem>

<Theorem kind="definition" n="5.2" name="Sliding Window CV" id="def-sliding-cv">

Like expanding window, but with fixed training size $w$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Fold } k: \quad &\text{Train} = \{n_k - w + 1, \ldots, n_k\} \\
                      &\text{Test} = \{n_k + g + 1, \ldots, n_k + g + m\}
\end{aligned}
$$

The window "slides" forward, discarding old data to maintain adaptivity to regime changes.

</Theorem>

<Theorem kind="definition" n="5.3" name="Purged Walk-Forward CV" id="def-purged-cv">

Following de Prado (<Cite key="dePrado2018" />), for data with overlapping labels or leakage concerns, a forward-only walk with purging:

1. Walk test windows forward through time, training only on observations *before* each test window.
2. **Purge** the last $g$ training observations adjacent to the test boundary (the `purge_gap`), so labels that overlap the test window never enter training.

</Theorem>

<Theorem kind="remark">

De Prado's original formulation is a *bidirectional* purged K-fold (training on both sides of each test fold, with a post-test embargo). For nuisance estimation in temporal DML that bidirectionality is itself lookahead: an earlier revision of this companion's `purged_cv` strategy trained nuisances on observations after the test window, and was replaced by the forward-only walk above.

</Theorem>

### Python implementation

Our `TimeSeriesCrossValidator` handles all three strategies:

```python
from temporalcv import TimeSeriesCrossValidator

# Expanding window with 5 folds and 2-period gap
cv = TimeSeriesCrossValidator(
    n_splits=5,
    gap=2,          # Periods between train end and test start
    expanding=True, # True for expanding, False for sliding
    min_train_size=50
)

# Generate train/test indices
for train_idx, test_idx in cv.split(X):
    print(f"Train: {train_idx[:5]}...{train_idx[-5:]}")
    print(f"Test:  {test_idx[:5]}...{test_idx[-5:]}")
```

<Theorem kind="remark">

The `gap` parameter is critical for applications where predictions take time to materialize (e.g., quarterly economic forecasts, insurance claims with reporting lag). Setting `gap=1` prevents any overlap between training and testing periods.

</Theorem>

## HAC standard errors

### The autocorrelation problem

For the population influence score, let $v_t$ be the treatment residual after conditioning on the controls, and let $\varepsilon_t$ be the structural outcome error. Then $\psi_t=v_t\varepsilon_t/\mathbb{E}[v_t^2]$. Let $n$ be the number of observations with out-of-fold residuals. The sample counterpart used for inference is

$$
\hat\psi_t = \frac{(\tilde{Y}_t - \hat{\tau}\tilde{T}_t)\tilde{T}_t}{n^{-1}\sum_s \tilde{T}_s^2}.
$$

<Theorem kind="theorem" n="5.1" name="HAC Variance of DML Estimator" id="thm-hac-variance">

Under regularity conditions, the DML estimator satisfies:

$$
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\tau} - \tau_0) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \Omega)
$$

where $\Omega$ is the **long-run variance**:

$$
\Omega = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{Cov}(\psi_0, \psi_k) = \gamma_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \gamma_k
$$

with $\gamma_k = \mathrm{Cov}(\psi_t, \psi_{t-k})$ the autocovariance at lag $k$.

</Theorem>

An interval based on the single-period score variance uses only $\gamma_0$. When the sum of positive-lag score autocovariances is positive, this understates the long-run variance. A negative sum has the opposite effect. If that sum is zero, the single-period and long-run variances coincide. The outcome error's autocorrelation alone does not determine the sign or magnitude of this adjustment.

### Newey–West estimation

We cannot sum to infinity, so we truncate with a kernel weighting scheme:

<Theorem kind="definition" n="5.4" name="Newey-West HAC Estimator" id="def-newey-west">

Following Newey and West (<Cite key="newey1987" />), the HAC variance estimator is:

$$
\hat{\Omega}_{NW} = \hat{\gamma}_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{B} w_k \hat{\gamma}_k
$$

where:

- $\hat{\gamma}_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=k+1}^{n} (\hat\psi_t-\bar\psi)(\hat\psi_{t-k}-\bar\psi)$ is the sample autocovariance of the fitted scores in time order, where $n$ counts the scored dates,
- $w_k$ is a kernel weight. For Bartlett, the implementation uses $w_k=1-k/(B+1)$ at lags $1,\ldots,B$,
- $B$ is the bandwidth (truncation parameter).

</Theorem>

### Kernel functions

Three commonly used kernels (implemented upstream in `temporalcv`):

1. **Bartlett (triangular)**:

$$
K_{\text{Bartlett}}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 - |x| & |x| \leq 1 \\ 0 & |x| > 1 \end{cases}
$$

Linear downweighting; guaranteed positive semi-definite.

2. **Parzen**:

$$
K_{\text{Parzen}}(x) = \begin{cases}
1 - 6x^2 + 6|x|^3 & |x| \leq 0.5 \\
2(1-|x|)^3 & 0.5 < |x| \leq 1 \\
0 & |x| > 1
\end{cases}
$$

Smoother than Bartlett; used for higher-order accuracy.

3. **Quadratic spectral**:

$$
K_{\text{QS}}(x) = \frac{25}{12\pi^2 x^2}\left(\frac{\sin(6\pi x/5)}{6\pi x/5} - \cos(6\pi x/5)\right)
$$

Optimal for minimizing MSE; non-truncating.

**Comparison of HAC kernel functions** — all decay to zero as lag increases, but with different rates and smoothness:

| Bartlett | Parzen | Quadratic Spectral |
|---|---|---|
| Linear decay | Smooth decay | Optimal MSE |

### Bandwidth selection

The bandwidth $B$ controls the bias–variance tradeoff:

- $B$ too small: misses important autocovariances (bias).
- $B$ too large: includes noise from distant lags (variance).

<Theorem kind="definition" n="5.5" name="Default Bartlett Bandwidth" id="def-optimal-bandwidth">

With `bandwidth="auto"`, `temporalcv` uses the number of scored dates $n$ to choose

$$
B_{\mathrm{auto}}=\left\lfloor n^{1/3}\right\rfloor.
$$

For the 415 scored dates in the simulations, this rule gives seven lags.

</Theorem>

The optional Andrews (1991) AR(1) plug-in uses the sample lag-one correlation $\hat\rho$ of the ordered influence scores. For the Bartlett kernel, the package computes

$$
\hat\alpha_1=\frac{4\hat\rho^2}{(1-\hat\rho^2)^2},
\qquad
B_{\mathrm{Andrews}}=\left\lfloor 1.1447\,(n\hat\alpha_1)^{1/3}\right\rfloor.
$$

The implementation clips $\hat\rho$ to $[-0.99,0.99]$ before computing the plug-in and caps the bandwidth at $n-1$.

### Python implementation

```python
from temporalcv import newey_west_se

result = newey_west_se(
    influence_scores,
    bandwidth="auto",   # floor(n^(1/3)), where n counts the scored dates
    kernel="bartlett",
)

# HACResult separates the three scales explicitly:
print(f"Long-run variance (Omega):      {result.long_run_variance:.4f}")
print(f"Variance of the mean (Omega/n): {result.variance:.4f}")
print(f"HAC SE = sqrt(Omega/n):         {result.se:.4f}")
print(f"Bandwidth used:                 {result.bandwidth}")
```

<Theorem kind="remark">

The explicit `long_run_variance` / `variance` / `se` split exists precisely because conflating $\Omega$ with $\Omega/n$ produces silent $\sqrt{n}$-scale errors in downstream standard errors; an earlier revision of this companion's estimator divided by $n$ twice at exactly this seam.

</Theorem>

## The TemporalPLRDML framework

### Algorithm overview

TemporalPLRDML combines time series cross-validation with HAC inference:

```
Algorithm: TemporalPLRDML Estimation
Require: Outcome Y, Treatment T, Confounders X, number of lags L
Ensure: Treatment effect τ̂ with HAC standard error

 1. Create lagged features:  X̃_t = (X_t, T_{t-1}, ..., T_{t-L})
 2. Initialize: time series cross-validator with gap g
 3. for each fold k = 1, ..., K:
 4.     Train outcome model   m̂^(-k) on training data
 5.     Train treatment model ℓ̂^(-k) on training data
 6.     Predict on test fold:  Ŷ^(-k)_t, T̂^(-k)_t
 7. Exclude uncovered early rows: drop observations without valid
    temporal out-of-fold predictions
 8. Compute residuals:  Ỹ_t = Y_t - Ŷ^(-t)_t,  T̃_t = T_t - T̂^(-t)_t
 9. Estimate τ:  τ̂ = (Σ_t Ỹ_t T̃_t) / (Σ_t T̃_t²)
10. Compute fitted scores:  ψ̂_t = (Ỹ_t - τ̂ T̃_t) T̃_t / mean(T̃²) on scored dates
11. HAC variance:  Ω̂ = γ̂_0 + 2 Σ_{k=1}^B w_k γ̂_k
12. Standard error:  SE(τ̂) = sqrt(Ω̂ / n_used), where n_used counts scored dates
return τ̂, SE(τ̂)
```

### Implementation

```python
from dml_ts import TemporalPLRDML
import numpy as np

# Generate time series data with autocorrelation
np.random.seed(42)
n = 500
time = np.arange(n)

# Confounders with temporal structure
X = np.column_stack([
    np.random.randn(n),
    np.sin(2 * np.pi * time / 100)  # Seasonal
])

# Autocorrelated treatment
T = np.zeros(n)
T[0] = np.random.randn()
for t in range(1, n):
    T[t] = 0.3 * T[t-1] + 0.5 * X[t, 0] + np.random.randn()

# Outcome with true effect tau = 2.0
true_tau = 2.0
Y = true_tau * T + X[:, 0]**2 + np.random.randn(n)

# Fit TemporalPLRDML
model = TemporalPLRDML(
    n_lags=3,
    model_y="random_forest",
    model_t="random_forest",
    cv_strategy="time_series_split",
    hac_kernel="bartlett",
    random_state=42,
)

result = model.fit(Y, T, X, time_index=time)
print(result.summary())
```

## Monte Carlo validation

Across repeated datasets with known slope $\tau_0=2$, we measure the mean estimation error (bias), compare the standard deviation of the estimates with the mean reported standard error, and count how often the nominal 95% interval contains the true slope.

### Data generating process

```python
def generate_ts_dgp(n, tau=2.0, rho_T=0.3, rho_eps=0.2, seed=None):
    """
    Generate time series DGP with autocorrelated treatment and errors.

    Parameters
    ----------
    n : int
        Sample size
    tau : float
        True treatment effect
    rho_T : float
        AR(1) coefficient for treatment
    rho_eps : float
        AR(1) coefficient for outcome error

    Returns
    -------
    Y, T, X, time : arrays
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    time = np.arange(n)

    # Confounders
    X = rng.standard_normal((n, 3))

    # AR(1) treatment
    T = np.zeros(n)
    T[0] = rng.standard_normal()
    for t in range(1, n):
        T[t] = rho_T * T[t-1] + 0.5 * X[t, 0] + rng.standard_normal()

    # AR(1) errors
    eps = np.zeros(n)
    eps[0] = rng.standard_normal()
    for t in range(1, n):
        eps[t] = rho_eps * eps[t-1] + rng.standard_normal()

    # Outcome
    Y = tau * T + X[:, 0]**2 + 0.5 * X[:, 1] + eps

    return Y, T, X, time
```

### A positive control with persistent scores

For the chapter generator, conditioning on $X_t$ and $T_{t-1}$ leaves a fresh, mean-zero treatment innovation $v_t$. This innovation is independent of all outcome errors and of earlier treatment innovations. At positive lags, the score autocovariance is proportional to $\mathbb E[v_t v_{t-k}\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-k}]=0$, even though the outcome errors have AR(1) coefficient 0.2.

For the second design, draw two independent standard normal covariates per date, independent of the treatment residual $u_t$ and the outcome error $e_t$. These residuals follow independent stationary Gaussian AR(1) processes with mean zero and unit variance, each with coefficient 0.8 and innovation standard deviation 0.6. Set

$$
T_t=X_{1t}+u_t, \qquad Y_t=2T_t+X_{2t}+e_t.
$$

Conditioning on the current covariates gives the population score $u_t e_t$. Its lag-$k$ correlation is $0.8^k\,0.8^k=0.64^k$. Summing over lags gives $\Omega/\gamma_0=(1+0.64)/(1-0.64)\approx 4.56$, so the asymptotic standard error is about $\sqrt{4.56}\approx 2.13$ times the lag-zero value.

### Simulation results

Each design generated 500 observations per independent replication. The chapter design above used seeds 0–199. The shuffled baseline used five folds, three current controls and 100-tree random forests with seed 42. The forward forest fit added one lag of treatment, used five expanding splits with gap zero, and scored 415 observations. In the persistent-score design (seeds 10000–10199), the forward fit used ridge regression, two current controls and no treatment lags. Within each forward design we used the same estimates and scores to compute both a zero-lag standard error and Bartlett HAC with automatic bandwidth $B=\lfloor 415^{1/3}\rfloor=7$.

## Practical recommendations

### When to use TemporalPLRDML

1. **Time series data**: any setting with temporal ordering.
2. **Serially correlated influence scores.** Inspect the time-ordered scores to assess the dependence relevant to inference.
3. **Lagged treatment controls**: when prior treatments are confounders or state variables for the current scalar effect.
4. **Macro/finance applications**: interest rates, prices, policies over time.

### Configuration guidelines

**TemporalPLRDML configuration guide**

| Parameter | Default | Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| `n_lags` | 1 | Increase for longer-memory effects |
| `cv_strategy` | time_series_split | Use purged_cv for financial data |
| `gap` | 0 | Set to forecast horizon |
| `hac_bandwidth` | auto | Manual for specific autocorrelation patterns |
| `hac_kernel` | bartlett | parzen for smoother estimates |

### Diagnostic checks

Before reporting results, verify:

1. **Nuisance model fit**: check cross-validated $R^2$ for both outcome and treatment models. Poor fit ($R^2 < 0.1$) suggests missing confounders or model misspecification.
2. **Score autocorrelation.** Plot the autocorrelation function of the fitted out-of-fold influence scores in time order. Inspect lags beyond the chosen bandwidth for dependence the estimator may miss.
3. **Bandwidth sensitivity.** Recompute the standard error from the same scores at bandwidth zero and across a range of positive bandwidths. Report the kernel, bandwidth and number of scored observations.

### Exercise 5.1: time series cross-validation

Consider a dataset with $n=100$ observations indexed $t=1,\ldots,100$. You want to use 5-fold time series CV with a gap of 2 periods.

(a) What are the train and test indices for each fold using expanding window CV?
(b) How many observations are in each test fold?
(c) Why is the gap parameter important for forecasting applications?

**Solution.**

(a) With 5 folds and gap=2:

- Fold 1: Train $\{1,\ldots,16\}$, Test $\{19,\ldots,36\}$ (after gap).
- Fold 2: Train $\{1,\ldots,34\}$, Test $\{37,\ldots,54\}$.
- …continuing the pattern.

(b) Each test fold contains approximately $n/K \approx 20$ observations (exact depends on rounding).

(c) The gap prevents data leakage when there's a delay between when information is available and when predictions are needed (e.g., quarterly economic data released with lag).

### Exercise 5.2: HAC bandwidth selection

Suppose the 415 scored dates in a forward fit have sample lag-one score correlation $\hat\rho=0.4$.

(a) Calculate the default Bartlett bandwidth.
(b) Calculate the optional Andrews plug-in bandwidth for the Bartlett kernel.
(c) How would you assess the effect of this choice on the reported standard error?

**Solution.**

(a) $B_{\mathrm{auto}}=\lfloor 415^{1/3}\rfloor=\lfloor 7.459\ldots\rfloor=7$.

(b) $\hat\alpha_1=4(0.4)^2/(1-0.4^2)^2\approx0.9070$, so $B_{\mathrm{Andrews}}=\lfloor 1.1447\,(415\hat\alpha_1)^{1/3}\rfloor=\lfloor 8.265\ldots\rfloor=8$.

(c) Compute the Bartlett standard errors at bandwidths seven and eight using the same fitted scores. Their difference measures sensitivity to bandwidth for this fit.

### Exercise 5.3: comparing standard errors

A researcher estimates $\hat{\tau} = 1.5$ using DML and reports two standard errors: an i.i.d. influence function SE of $0.10$ and a HAC (Newey–West) SE of $0.18$.

(a) Compute the 95% confidence intervals under each assumption.
(b) What does the difference in SEs tell you about the data?
(c) Which CI should be reported in a time series setting?

**Solution.**

(a) i.i.d.: $1.5 \pm 1.96 \times 0.10 = [1.30, 1.70]$. HAC: $1.5 \pm 1.96 \times 0.18 = [1.15, 1.85]$.

(b) For the same scores, the larger HAC SE implies a positive weighted sum of the estimated score autocovariances over the included lags. Individual lag autocovariances can have either sign.

(c) If the design supports weakly dependent scores, report the HAC interval $[1.15, 1.85]$ along with the kernel, bandwidth and a bandwidth sensitivity check.

## Summary

This chapter extended DML to time series settings through three key innovations:

1. **Time series cross-validation**: expanding/sliding windows with purging prevent data leakage while maintaining temporal ordering.
2. **HAC standard errors.** Newey–West estimates the long-run variance of the influence scores. Interval coverage also depends on estimator bias, bandwidth and sample size.
3. **TemporalPLRDML framework**: integrates lagged controls, temporal CV, and HAC inference into a scalar PLR estimation procedure.

**Key takeaways:**

- Standard random-fold DML can create future-data leakage and misleading uncertainty for ordered data.
- Time series CV with gaps prevents future information leakage.
- Compare zero-lag and HAC standard errors on the same out-of-fold scores. Their difference depends on the weighted sum of score autocovariances.
- TemporalPLRDML reports lag rows and temporal-CV rows dropped before the final residual regression.

## Roadmap to Chapter 6

Chapter 6 extends our time series framework in two directions:

1. **Panel DML**: combining fixed effects with DML for panel data (repeated cross-sections over time).
2. **Rolling Window DML**: estimating time-varying treatment effects when the causal relationship is non-stationary.

These methods are essential for applications where treatment effects may differ across individuals or change over time — common in insurance pricing, macroeconomic policy, and financial markets.
