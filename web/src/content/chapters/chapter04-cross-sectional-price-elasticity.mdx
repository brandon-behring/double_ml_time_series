# Cross-Sectional Application: Price Elasticity with Sensitivity Analysis

## Introduction

Chapters 1–3 established the foundations of double machine learning (DML) and used simulations where the true treatment effect is known. Here we apply the same partially linear estimator to orange juice sales at Dominick's Finer Foods. The price and sales data are observed together. How a store chose its prices matters for interpreting the result.

A pricing analyst asks what will happen to a brand's sales if the store raises its price by 1%. Two features of the data shape our answer. A premium brand can sell in different quantities from a store brand even at the same price. And when a chain runs a promotion, dozens of store observations can reflect one decision. Accounting for brand changes the fitted slope. Accounting for the shared promotion changes how much independent information supports it.

## The Orange Juice Dataset

### Data description

The extract contains **28,947 store–brand–week observations**, with three brands at each of 9,649 observed store–weeks. There are 83 stores and 121 weeks, numbered 40 through 160. All three brands have 9,649 observations. A complete rectangle would contain 10,043 store–weeks. The sample weights each observed brand record equally.

The outcome is `logmove`, the log of units sold. The exposure is log shelf price. The original adjustment uses the advertising feature indicator `feat` and two neighborhood demographics, `INCOME` and `AGE60`. In the brand-adjusted fit, the nuisance regressions also receive indicators for Minute Maid and Tropicana, with Dominick's as the reference brand. All rows remain in the pooled analysis.

We acknowledge the Kilts Center for Marketing at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business for the Dominick's data. The public scanner archive is much larger than the three-brand teaching extract. The replication uses a fixed revision of the extract and verifies the file digest. [Kilts Center data documentation](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/research/kilts/research-data/dominicks) · [Teaching extract](https://github.com/gchoi/Dataset/blob/8e81d46872564218a78be95d53a9c6bd3b00b199/oj.csv)

### The causal question

For observation $j=(s,b,w)$ with store $s$, brand $b$ and week $w$, consider the partially linear regression

$$
Y_j = \theta T_j + g(X_j) + \varepsilon_j.
$$

A coefficient of $-1.17$ means that a 1% higher price is associated with approximately 1.17% lower sales after the specified adjustment. With a common structural slope, adequate price variation at each relevant covariate value, and mean independence of the remaining demand shock and price given those covariates, the slope can represent a causal own-price elasticity. In that interpretation the prices of competing brands and the timing of the featured advertisement must also be accounted for by the design.

For example, a manager expecting unusually strong weekend demand might set both a higher price and a larger display. Current prices can also shift purchases between brands. The file gives no assignment mechanism establishing that the remaining variation in price is exogenous. These regressions estimate conditional log-price slopes on the observed assortment.

## DML Price Elasticity Estimation

### The original five-fold fit

Use the two nuisance regressions to predict log sales and log price from the observed controls. Subtract each held-out prediction from the observed value, obtaining $\tilde Y_j$ and $\tilde T_j$. Pool the residuals over the scored rows and fit the slope through the origin.

$$
\hat\theta = \frac{\sum_j \tilde T_j\tilde Y_j}{\sum_j \tilde T_j^2}.
$$

```python
from dml_ts import double_ml

result = double_ml(
    Y=data.Y, T=data.T, X=data.X, n_folds=5,
    model="random_forest", random_state=42,
)
print(result.theta, result.se)
```

The literal chapter call returns **−1.1673** with a row-independent SE of **0.0158**. The outcome and price nuisance fits have held-out $R^2$ of 0.352 and 0.092. Each forest has 100 trees, maximum depth five, and minimum leaf size ten. Both learner and shuffled-fold seeds are 42. The archived CSV order determines the shuffled split.

### Which observations move together?

The normalized influence score of row $j$ measures its contribution to uncertainty in the estimated slope,

$$
\hat\psi_j =
\frac{\tilde T_j(\tilde Y_j-\hat\theta\tilde T_j)}
{n^{-1}\sum_k\tilde T_k^2}.
$$

First keep every estimate, prediction and score fixed. For a partition into $G$ groups, the scalar cluster variance below allows arbitrary covariance within each group. The factor $G/(G-1)$ is the finite-cluster correction used in these comparisons.

$$
\hat V_{g} = \frac{G}{G-1}\frac{1}{n^2}
    \sum_{a=1}^{G}\left(\sum_{j \in a}\hat\psi_j\right)^2.
$$

**Uncertainty for the same pooled slope of −1.1673**

| Dependence | Groups | SE | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Independent rows | 28,947 | 0.0158 | [−1.1983, −1.1362] |
| Store | 83 | 0.0710 | [−1.3064, −1.0282] |
| Week | 121 | 0.0945 | [−1.3525, −0.9821] |
| Store and week | 83 and 121 | 0.1171 | [−1.3969, −0.9377] |

Stores can exhibit persistent promotional, inventory or local demand shocks, so repeated observations at a store can covary across weeks. A holiday or chain-wide advertisement can make scores at different stores covary in one week. Two-way clustering adds the store and week variance estimates and subtracts the store–week intersection. Each intersection contains three brand records. Subtracting a row-level variance would give a different answer. [Cameron, Gelbach and Miller](https://doi.org/10.1198/jbes.2010.07136)

The two-way SE is about 7.4 times the row-independent SE. These are large-sample normal intervals, conditional on the chosen fit and its sampling assumptions. The two-way calculation assumes observations sharing neither store nor week have negligible covariance. A nationwide shock lasting several weeks could violate that assumption. In a setting with persistent common shocks, Chapter 5's HAC calculation can be applied to the successive sums of weekly influence scores with the appropriate observation weights.

The following calculation reproduces the clustered SEs on the pooled fit. The zero-intercept final regression has one residualized price coefficient.

```python
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.stats.sandwich_covariance import cov_cluster_2groups

final = sm.OLS(result.Y_residual, result.T_residual,
               hasconst=False).fit()
both, store, week = cov_cluster_2groups(
    final, frame["store"].to_numpy(), frame["week"].to_numpy()
)
print([np.sqrt(v.item()) for v in (store, week, both)])
```

## Baseline Comparison

### OLS and the shuffled forest

Across all brands and dates, OLS of log sales on log price with an intercept gives **−1.6013**. Adding the three original controls linearly gives **−1.1697**. Replacing the linear nuisance regressions with the shuffled forest gives **−1.1673**. The observed change on adding controls moves the slope toward zero. The data do not reveal which of these slopes is closest to a causal elasticity.

### Brand adjustment and forecast timing

Compare two feature sets under two fold designs. Shuffled folds hold out individual records, so other brands from the same store and week, and later weeks, can appear in training. Forward folds train on all observations through an earlier week and score every brand and store in the next block. Each fit uses a zero-week gap. The first forward split trains on weeks 40–60 and scores weeks 61–80. The remaining four splits each add 20 weeks to training and score the next 20. Within each scored window, the model fitted at the start supplies every prediction.

**Pooled forest fits by fold design and brand adjustment**

| Folds | Brand indicators | Rows | Weeks | Slope | Two-way SE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Shuffled | No | 28,947 | 121 | −1.1673 | 0.1171 |
| Shuffled | Yes | 28,947 | 121 | −2.6289 | 0.1659 |
| Forward | No | 24,108 | 100 | −1.3646 | 0.1202 |
| Forward | Yes | 24,108 | 100 | −2.6117 | 0.1467 |

The forward fits score 24,108 rows in 8,036 store–weeks across all 83 stores and 100 weeks. Weeks 40–60 contribute 4,839 training-only rows. Within a fold design, comparing the two feature sets holds the scored sample fixed. Including brand moves the slope substantially in both designs. The premium and store brands have different price levels and sales patterns; allowing the forests to condition on brand changes the price and sales variation entering the slope.

Comparing across fold designs changes both the information available for training and the observations scored. A follow-up can restrict both final regressions to the same weeks to study how much of the difference is due to the scoring population. Forward evaluation addresses a later-week prediction question. The store/week variance comparison addresses dependence in the resulting scores. For a formal multiway DML design, Chiang and coauthors also partition the nuisance-training sample along the clustering dimensions. [Chiang, Kato, Ma and Sasaki](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.2021.1895815)

## Overlap Diagnostics

For a continuous price, the denominator of the partially linear score depends on residual price variation. A useful diagnostic asks which prices occur across stores and weeks after adjustment. For the original shuffled forest, the within-store SD of residual log price ranges from **0.228 to 0.303**, and the within-week SD ranges from **0.052 to 0.538**. The small value in one week illustrates what the pooled SD can hide.

```python
diagnostics = frame[["store", "brand", "week"]].copy()
diagnostics["residual_price"] = result.T_residual
for key in ("store", "week"):
    s = diagnostics.groupby(key)["residual_price"].std()
    print(key, s.min(), s.max())
```

These marginal ranges can guide inspection of sparse store, brand and price combinations. Positivity for a proposed price change is local to comparable observations. To study an increase from a specific shelf price, check whether nearby higher prices occur in the same brand, promotion status and period. Trimming a low-support segment changes the population being described. A constant propensity assigned after splitting prices at the median carries no information about conditional support.

## Sensitivity to Unmeasured Confounding

A narrower confidence interval quantifies sampling uncertainty under a model. An omitted promotion calendar or a manager's demand forecast can systematically shift the slope regardless of the interval width. One empirical comparison is to add measured brand and examine the result on identical rows. This establishes sensitivity to brand composition; it gives no bound on the bias from unrecorded demand shocks.

Rosenbaum's $\Gamma$ sensitivity model concerns differences in the odds of binary treatment assignment within matched sets. A formal application must define the treatment contrast and matched design. The E-value describes confounder associations on a risk-ratio scale. Weekly log units sold is a continuous outcome, and exponentiating its log-price coefficient gives a ratio of sales for a specified price change under the model. Calling that number an event risk ratio would assign an outcome the study never measured. [Rosenbaum](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4757-3692-2) · [VanderWeele and Ding](https://doi.org/10.7326/M16-2607)

Partial identification would require an explicit constraint on how strongly a latent demand shock could jointly influence prices and sales. A defensible sensitivity exercise could specify the size and timing of unobserved promotional spending, calibrate those assumptions against a measured promotion, and show the range of slopes across those scenarios. An identified set or an assignment-odds threshold would require additional design assumptions.

## Gasoline Price Elasticity in a Simulation

The gasoline exercise provides a known coefficient. Draw independent income, urban density and crude oil variables. Retail price rises with crude oil cost and income. In the log-demand equation, crude oil also has a direct negative coefficient. Omitting oil then leaves price variation related to the demand error. The numbers are generated for this exercise.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.RandomState(42)
n = 5000
income = rng.normal(50, 15, n)
urban = rng.normal(0.6, 0.2, n)
oil = rng.normal(80, 20, n)
price = (2.50 + 0.015 * oil + 0.005 * income
         + rng.normal(0, 0.15, n))
t = np.log(price)
y = (3.0 - 0.35 * t + 0.008 * income - 0.3 * urban
     - 0.004 * oil + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n))
for features in [(income, urban), (income, urban, oil)]:
    fit = double_ml(y, t, np.column_stack(features),
                    n_folds=5, model="random_forest",
                    random_state=42)
    print(fit.theta, fit.se)
```

Using the same 5,000 observations and shuffled five-fold forests, income and urban density alone yield **−1.1683** (SE 0.0492). Adding the observed simulation oil variable gives **−0.5173** (SE 0.1083). Here the true coefficient is −0.35 by construction. The differences from truth, −0.8183 and −0.1673, are errors on a single draw. Estimating simulation bias or interval coverage would require repeated independent draws. For Dominick's, the corresponding true slope is unknown.

Repeat the original shuffled fit separately by brand and by neighborhood income. The income split uses the sample median of the `INCOME` column across the 28,947 records, assigning ties to the lower group. Each regression uses its own nuisance fits and residual price variation. The intervals use that subgroup's store/week clusters.

## Diagnostic Visualization

Which stores and dates contribute most to the uncertainty? A residual-price histogram shows the range used for the fit. A plot of **weekly score sums** highlights weeks in which many observations push the estimated slope in the same direction. For the original shuffled fit the scores align with the original rows.

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

diagnostics["score"] = result.influence_scores
week_score = diagnostics.groupby("week")["score"].sum()
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
axes[0].hist(diagnostics["residual_price"], bins=60)
axes[0].set_xlabel("Residual log price")

week_score.plot(ax=axes[1])
```

Inspect unusually large weekly sums alongside the promotion calendar and recorded prices. Removing an influential week should be reported as a change in the estimand's sample. Symmetry of a histogram and a good first-stage fit cannot establish that all pricing confounders were measured.

## Chapter Summary

The original shuffled fit estimates a pooled conditional log-price slope of −1.1673. Its store/week SE is 0.1171. The brand-adjusted forward fit gives −2.6117 on 100 scored weeks.
