# What survives rolling validation in two pricing experiments?

Bangalore and London both supply evidence that prices changed behavior over the study as a whole. The later data support different conclusions. In Bangalore there are too few commuters in the final months to distinguish persistence from a change in effect. In London the final quarter has no high- and low-price events at the same start time. Even an arbitrarily large number of meters on those dates would leave the matched contrast unidentified within that quarter. [Bangalore report](bangalore/README.md) · [London report](london/README.md)

| Question | Bangalore congestion pricing | London time-of-use electricity prices |
|---|---|---|
| What was assigned? | An individual commuter’s pricing arm. Repeated GPS days describe that commuter’s behavior. | A supply-following price schedule on an event day, shared by households that opted into the tariff. |
| What does the full-study estimate measure? | High-rate assignment changed the normalized daily hypothetical charge rate by **−12.94 points** (commuter-clustered SE **6.04**). The published analysis used **497 commuters** and **15,574 days**. | High-price versus low-price three-hour events changed average consumption by **−0.209 kWh per participating household** (approximate date-based 95% interval **−0.397 to −0.020**), equally weighting eight start times. |
| What did later observations show? | August’s history-adjusted change was **−0.69** with SE **20.39**, from **20 high-rate and 18 control commuters**. September supplied no high-rate observations. | The full year had three high and three low days at each start time. In Q4 the high days began at 02:00 and 20:00, and the low days began at other hours. |
| Which assumptions matter most? | The published regression and monthly participant-average changes weight people differently. GPS availability was about 76% in each arm. The composition of missing days can still matter. | The estimate concerns price during events among enrolled households. It depends on the mapping of local event times to meter interval ends and on comparability of the selected event dates. |

The Bangalore replay matched the **published** sample size, control mean, coefficient and SE from original records. Baseline-history adjustment reduced the full-sample SE by 4.3%. The rolling ridge model predicted control outcomes better than a zero-change forecast in only one of five evaluable months. Months reuse some commuters. Counts of significant months would mix changes in precision, composition and response. [Published reconstruction and monthly results](bangalore/results.json) · [Published study](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18422)

The London calendar supplies **48 assignments** for this comparison within 167.9 million source meter readings. Using a balanced group of 982 tariff households gave **−0.214 kWh**. Under alternative timestamp alignments the high-minus-low estimate remained negative, but its interval included zero. To check uncertainty against known effects, we also assigned prices to dates on observed standard-tariff household histories. With 1,024 households and 48 dates, date-based intervals covered **94.2%** of the time, and intervals treating household-days as independent covered **25.2%**. Both imposed effects, zero and −0.18 kWh, gave these coverage rates. [Meter estimates](london/effects.csv) · [Calibration and Monte Carlo SEs](london/calibration.csv) · [Trial design](https://doi.org/10.25560/25575)

For these studies, a defensible forward analysis begins by counting assigned commuters or price days and identifying the comparisons available at each origin. An outcome predictor earns its place by improving held-out prediction or the precision of a prespecified causal contrast. Testing whether an earlier effect carries forward additionally requires treatment variation in the later period, a common estimand, and uncertainty at the assignment level.
