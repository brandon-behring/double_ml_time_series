# How often does a rolling comparison detect a change?

In 500 paired replications using the reminder enrollment calendar, a stable effect triggered at least one nominal 5% alarm across the four informative quarters **95 times (19.0%, Monte Carlo SE 1.76 percentage points)** using the same refitted participant bootstrap as the real study. Looking only at the two prespecified post-change dates, **50/500** panels signaled under stability and **475/500** signaled when the true effect fell by 0.10 log units. An alert seen anywhere on a rolling plot and an alert at a single prespecified decision date have different false-positive rates. [End-to-end results](bootstrap-calibration.csv) · [Empirical reminder report](reminders.md)

The faster analytical interval raised at least one alarm in **75/500 stable panels (15.0%, Monte Carlo SE 1.60 points)**. Its individual false-alarm rates ranged from 2.8% to 5.0%. The following comparisons give results separately for the two interval procedures. ## A known change at the January 2023 decision date

We also reran the **actual empirical percentile interval** for the stable and declining scenarios. Each of 500 replications refitted the nuisance models with 999 joint participant-weight draws, using the same people across their scheduled dates. In chronological order, under stability its false-alarm rates were **4.8%, 6.0%, 4.0%, and 6.4%**. For a decline of 0.10 it raised an alarm in **404/500 January** and **416/500 April** comparisons (80.8% and 83.2%, Monte Carlo SEs 1.76 and 1.67 points). In the two post-break periods its 95% intervals covered the true early-minus-later difference in 96.0% and 93.6% of draws. The calendar, shocks, participant folds and assignment draws match the analytical calibration below. [End-to-end bootstrap](../external/forward/reminder_bootstrap_calibration.py) · [Results for all four dates](bootstrap-calibration.csv)

Here an alarm means that a two-sided, pointwise 95% interval for the historical forecast minus the later estimate excludes zero. Coverage asks whether the same interval contains the known difference between the earlier and later population targets. MSE measures the frozen forecast against the known later effect. It is expressed in squared log units. All 500 AIPW comparisons had sufficient support for an interval at this date.

| Scenario and comparison | Alarms | Coverage | Mean forecast error against later truth | Forecast MSE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Stable +0.12, AIPW | 2.8% | 97.2% | +0.00045 | 0.00041 |
| Decline from +0.12 to +0.02, AIPW | 79.0% | 97.2% | +0.10045 | 0.01050 |
| Changing assignment, native PLR | 100.0% | 99.0% | +0.18430 | 0.03429 |
| Changing assignment, fixed subgroup mix | 3.4% | 96.6% | +0.00008 | 0.00036 |
| Stable +0.12 with very rare activity prompts, AIPW | 8.8% | 91.2% | −0.00047 | 0.04215 |

All table entries use the same 500 random seeds; individual rates have binomial Monte Carlo SEs. For January decline detection the SE is **1.82 percentage points**. The fixed-mix and native rows estimate different effects. Positive and negative subgroup responses of +0.20 and −0.20 stay unchanged. Swapping their assignment probabilities changes how much the native PLR coefficient weights each group. The average earlier native forecast was **+0.0760**, while the later native target was **−0.1083**. A fixed 50/50 population retained an effect of zero. Native PLR accurately detected movement in its own coefficient. Interpreting all 500 alarms as a change in subgroup response would have been wrong. [Estimand derivation](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/rssb.12504)

Weak support had a separate cost. In January AIPW's forecast MSE rose from **0.00041 to 0.04215** even though mean error remained close to zero. The fixed-mix score could be estimated in only 86.0%, 90.0%, 97.8%, and 86.8% of replications in the four evaluable quarters. The archive records failures as well as positive results. Its zero-forecast alarm rate measures evidence of an effect against zero, a different question from drift in a previous estimate.

## Calendar and data-generating process

- The source contributes the enrollment dates of 287 people, 78,812 eligible decision occasions, ages, baseline steps and pre-decision steps. We keep the number of decisions per participant, study week and calendar week. Within these cells we average covariates to make repeated fitting inexpensive. Outcomes and assignments are generated independently of the source's observed responses. The calendar includes seven starts, two with no mature training data and one with a single new participant. [Calendar and source filters](../external/forward/reminder_rolling.py)
- Training requires enrollment plus 98 days to precede issuance. The later population consists of new enrollees during the next quarter. Effects change with enrollment cohort in the decline scenario. Outcomes include fixed covariate effects, a participant shock with SD 0.7, a calendar-week shock with SD 0.25, and decision-level residual noise with SD 1. The two principal arms normally have probabilities 0.125 and 0.75. Diet receives the remainder. In the weak-support scenario the older group's activity probability is 0.0005.
- Historical arm differences use known-probability, self-normalized means. AIPW fits ridge outcome models with five whole-participant folds and the known probabilities. The native partially linear slope absorbs subgroup and assignment-period strata among activity and no-prompt assignments. The fixed-mix score gives the two age groups equal weight. Each method's oracle accounts for its population and weighting. A frozen zero has no training uncertainty.
- The analytical drift interval uses separate early and later participant contributions and the smaller available cluster degrees of freedom for the critical value. Calendar shocks are shared in the simulation. These intervals condition on the calendar and assess participant variation. A design with a common policy shock or several reused future cohorts requires appropriate inference at those levels. Repeated alarms are pointwise. The per-replication “any alarm” calculation retains their dependence.
- For each scenario the archive has 500 replications × 7 dates × 5 methods. The 70,000 rows include unsupported or point-only comparisons. Stable and decline scenarios share pre-break outcomes exactly. One worker and eight workers produced byte-identical compressed draws and summary CSVs, with one numerical-library thread per process.

The four actual reminder error intervals were **1.38 to 1.49 times as wide** as the average bootstrap intervals in the simulation. The observed widths in chronological order were 0.2971, 0.2497, 0.2043 and 0.2017 log units. The corresponding simulated mean widths were 0.2161, 0.1723, 0.1383 and 0.1355. The 80.8% and 83.2% post-change detection rates describe this data-generating process. Estimating power for another deployment requires a defensible noise level, enough independent assignments, and a realistic adoption window.
