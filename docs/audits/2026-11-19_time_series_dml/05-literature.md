# Literature gaps and benchmarks for temporal DML

The closest published alternative for the scalar time-series estimator is already in the repository. Ciganovic, D’Amario and Tancioni use reverse cross-fitting under assumptions that permit time-reversed training blocks. Their journal article appeared after the May dossier. A genuinely new addition, Yu, Chen, Du and Li, instead study horizon-specific effects with buffered folds and an instrument. For microeconomics, the most productive addition is a randomized treatment-path benchmark. It gives the panel or time-series estimate a design-based reference whose interpretation comes from the assignment mechanism. [Published scalar DML comparator](https://doi.org/10.1093/ectj/utag019) · [Buffered IV estimator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27531) · [Randomized panel benchmark](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE1744)

## Sources missing from the paper ledger

An applied complement to the temporal IV work is Angrist, Graddy and Imbens (2000) on identification in simultaneous supply and demand systems. Their whiting-market study explains what variation in observed prices can identify when buyers have heterogeneous responses. The original paper and the dated public teaching sample give Chapter 4 an alternative to scanner-price regressions and Chapter 5 a short series for checking instrument support by fold. [Published demand study](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937X.00141) · [File comparison and computed benchmarks](06-public-datasets.md#fulton-fish-market--a-short-demand-series-with-an-instrument)

The original dossier deliberately deferred synthetic controls, staggered difference-in-differences and mediation. Their relevance depends on treatment assignment. ## First three empirical comparisons

1. **Isolate the Chapter 5 variance claim.** Generate a constant-slope series with independent, centered stationary AR(1) treatment residuals and outcome errors, with coefficients `a` and `b`. Use zero treatment lags so the oracle residuals retain their AR dynamics. The population score has lag-`k` autocorrelation `(a*b)^k`, giving a known long-run variance multiplier `(1+a*b)/(1-a*b)` relative to its contemporaneous variance. Include a zero-persistence arm and compare identical scores across bandwidths. This directly checks a condition on the score. The original chapter DGP with at least one treatment lag supplies a second zero-score-autocorrelation control. [Chapter 5](../../../chapters/chapter_05.tex) · [Audit reproduction](04-reproducibility.md) · [Müller](https://www.princeton.edu/~umueller/HACtest.pdf)
2. **Establish a randomized reference.** Use repeated randomized treatment with a prespecified unit and carryover window. Report treatment probabilities and actual independent assignment counts. Compare the direct design-based contrast and temporal DML where both target the same quantity, with randomization inference for the sharp null. Repeated assignments or outcome simulations allow coverage measurement. On one realized experiment report differences, sampling uncertainty and sensitivity. [Panel randomization](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE1744) · [Switchback designs](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4583)
3. **Stress the retail-panel assumptions.** On a store-week panel, keep the same outcome, price contrast and controls while changing the split unit, time buffer and clustering dimensions. Add a semi-synthetic outcome with a known elasticity to distinguish failure of the estimator from disagreement about real-world demand. Product-specific responses, simultaneous pricing, promotion timing and common shocks remain economic identification questions in the observational run. [Scanner-price benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.09988) · [Multiway DML](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.03489) · [Manuscript findings](03-manuscript-and-content.md)

## Can successful field experiments overpredict savings when a program expands?

Allcott’s 2015 Opower study compares household energy reports in **111 randomized trials**, covering 8.6 million households at 58 utilities. Table VI uses the first ten sites to predict the average first-year electricity savings at the next 101. The later trials averaged **1.26%** after adjustment for mailing frequency. Linear and reweighted forecasts were **1.92%** and **1.66%**. The paper reports overpredictions of **0.66 and 0.41 percentage points** from the underlying unrounded estimates. Early utilities and the high-usage households they initially targeted were unusually responsive. Randomized assignment within each site supplied an effect estimate while program expansion changed which sites and households received the intervention. [Paper, Table VI](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Allcott_SiteSelectionBias.pdf) · [Published article](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv015)

The author’s replication package contains 65 files, including the Stata programs and instructions documenting restrictions on redistributing the Opower records. These are author-reported estimates. The published exercise also harmonizes mailing frequency across all 111 sites, so verifying what an operator could have estimated by a particular historical date would require dated release and program records. The retail benchmark offers a public counterpart with observable later randomized assignments, seven days of logged impressions and a much shorter horizon. [Author’s replication archive](https://dl.dropboxusercontent.com/s/v6bio96yba5bjd8/Allcott%20-%20Site%20Selection%20Bias%20-%20Code%20for%20Replication.zip) · [Retail benchmark](forward/retail-recommendations.md)
