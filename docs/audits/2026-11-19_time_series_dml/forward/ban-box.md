# Did an earlier randomized callback gap carry into the next application wave?

In New Jersey, applications with White-signaling names had a **4.77 percentage-point** callback advantage over applications with Black-signaling names in the earlier wave, and a **4.75-point** advantage in the later wave. In New York City, the corresponding differences were **0.26** and **0.97 points**. These estimates concern the particular application and employer samples. Agan and Starr randomly varied the name signal on otherwise constructed applications. The dates on which the jurisdictions adopted Ban the Box were set by policy. [Original paper, design and Table V](https://jenni.uchicago.edu/econ341/readings/Agan_Starr_2017_QJE_v133_n1.pdf) · [Data and Stata code](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/VPHMNT) · [Analysis](../external/forward/ban_box.py)

Each frozen prediction uses pre-policy applications within the paper's reported dates whose **eight weeks** of follow-up had elapsed before the first post-wave applications. Positive responses must have a dated callback within that window. We include all employer types and use no later information about which removed the criminal-history question or returned to the sample. A saturated name-by-wave regression recovers the four cell means in each jurisdiction. The interval for their difference clusters both waves together by chain, with a cluster degrees-of-freedom correction. There were 122 chains represented in both New Jersey waves and 238 in both New York waves. The comparison allows sample composition and conditions to change across waves.

**Operator and public clocks.** The operator could have reconstructed these estimates at the start of each post wave assuming callbacks were entered contemporaneously. The archive lacks entry timestamps. **No New Jersey pre-wave application had completed its eight weeks by the March 1 effective date.** The New Jersey prediction is feasible at the May 4 restart. All 4,300 eligible New York pre-wave applications could have matured by its October 27 effective date. The archived replication release is later than both cutoffs. A public analyst could have specified a zero-gap benchmark then. The deposits reviewed here provide no contemporaneous public outcome file. [Paper, section II](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx028) · [Author deposit](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/VPHMNT)

**The published policy comparison answers a different question.** Replaying Table V, column 1 gives **+3.898 points** (chain-clustered SE **1.982**) on 11,188 balanced applications across 223 chains. This is the change in the name gap at box-removing stores relative to its change at comparison stores, with education and employment-gap controls. A causal policy interpretation requires comparable trends across store categories. Replicating these cells checks the input and original analysis. Conditioning a historical prediction on realized box removal would use information learned in the later wave. [Table V and assumptions](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx028)

**Date check.** Across both waves one New Jersey and 201 New York application dates fall outside the published date ranges. One further New York positive callback has an earlier date than its application. These rows are omitted from the dated exercise. Using the archive's wave flags and response indicator for every row gives signed errors of +0.01 and −0.88 points. The published replay keeps the authors' exact sample and variables.
